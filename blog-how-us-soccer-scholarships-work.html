How US soccer scholarships work for UK & Irish players

28 September 2026 · 7 min read

Every year hundreds of UK and Irish players swap Saturday league football for a US college team, a degree and a full-time training environment. If you're a player or a parent wondering how that actually works, this guide covers the basics.

Why the US?

In the UK, most players have to choose between football and education by around 16. US college soccer lets you do both. You train and play in a structured, professional-style programme (daily sessions, strength and conditioning, physios, travel to away games) while earning a recognised degree.

College soccer is also a real pathway. Plenty of players go on to MLS, the USL or back to clubs in Europe, and the ones who don't finish with a degree and often very little debt.

Who offers scholarships?

There are several governing bodies, each with its own divisions and rules:

  • NCAA Division I: the biggest programmes and the highest level on average. Very competitive.
  • NCAA Division II: a strong standard, with a good balance of football and academics. Many UK players end up here.
  • NCAA Division III: no athletic scholarships, but many schools give generous academic and merit awards.
  • NAIA: smaller universities, a competitive level, and more flexible recruiting rules.
  • NJCAA (junior college): two-year colleges. A popular stepping stone before transferring to a four-year university.

We break these down properly in NCAA vs NAIA vs NJCAA: which one fits you.

What does a scholarship cover?

A "full ride" (tuition, accommodation, meals and books all paid) exists, but it's the exception, not the rule. Most soccer scholarships are partial. A coach splits a set budget across the squad, so you might be offered 40%, 60% or 80% of costs.

The good news is that athletic money can often be combined with academic scholarships. Strong grades can make a big difference to the final figure. That's why we always say your grades matter as much as your football.

When comparing offers, always look at the total cost per year after all aid, not the headline percentage. A 50% offer at a cheaper university can cost less than 70% at an expensive one.

What coaches look for

  • Level of play: academy, National League system, League of Ireland, strong school or college teams, and so on.
  • Footage: a good highlight reel plus full-match video. See what coaches look for in a highlight reel.
  • Academics: GCSEs, A-levels, Leaving Cert or equivalent. Better grades mean more options and more money.
  • Character: coaches will speak to you and often your current coach. Attitude and coachability count.

The process, step by step

  1. Get assessed honestly. Work out which divisions and levels are realistic for you.
  2. Build your profile. Highlight reel, full-match footage, playing CV, grades and references.
  3. Reach coaches. Targeted emails to programmes that fit your level, position and academics.
  4. Calls and offers. Video calls with interested coaches, then written offers.
  5. Eligibility. Register with the NCAA Eligibility Center or the NAIA Eligibility Center if needed, and send transcripts.
  6. Admission and signing. Apply to the university and sign your athletic aid agreement.
  7. Visa and travel. Your university issues an I-20, you pay the SEVIS fee and attend an F-1 visa interview (at the US Embassy in London, or in Dublin for Irish citizens).

Soccer is played in the autumn, so most players arrive in early August for pre-season. Some programmes also take January starters.

When should you start?

Earlier than most people think. Ideally around age 15–16, but it's rarely too late. Our recruiting timeline by age covers what to do at each stage.

A few honest warnings

  • Rules change. The NCAA in particular has changed scholarship and roster rules recently, so always confirm the details with the coach and university.
  • If you've already left school and are playing senior football, check how that affects your eligibility before assuming you have four years to play.
  • Be wary of anyone who guarantees a scholarship. Nobody can promise an offer. A good adviser tells you where you realistically stand.
